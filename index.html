The Perfect Session

40-minute structure and the 5 Core Session Rules. Strengths: delivering an enjoyable session and giving equal attention between members. Opportunities: feedback quality on each lift and equipment management.

Gym Floor Awareness

Every member on the floor — whether you're directly coaching them or not — should feel like a VIP inside Lockeroom. Members have right of way at all times. When you have spare time during a session, check in with the lead coach first before jumping in.

The Coach's Lens

5-phase framework: 1. Setup · 2. Initiation · 3. Movement Pattern · 4. Points of Performance · 5. Set Completion. Before coaching anything, identify which phase the issue is happening in. If Setup and Initiation are right, the rest usually follows.

The Cue Process

When something isn't working: Observe → Diagnose → Intervene → Adjust → Substitute. Go through 2–3 cycles before considering a swap. After an adjustment, ask: "Is that the same, better, or worse?"

Floor Habits

Equipment: When finished with loaded equipment (like the trap bar) — strip it straight away. Tell the client: "I'm just going to unpack this — I'll be with you in a second." Clients often help with plates; the key is you initiating.

Understanding Your Clients

Most Lockeroom clients are sedentary. Tight hips, hamstrings, lower back — and those areas are also weak. When you put them on the 45° back extension, they'll feel it in their lower back — that's expected. Your job: shift the load to glutes and hamstrings. The lower back stabilises; the glutes and hamstrings lift.

Tactile Cueing

Tactile cues draw attention — they're not about force. One finger, one point of contact. Your touch tells the client where to think; your verbal cue tells them what to do. For many clients, this is the first time they've felt what a muscle feels like under load.

Setup: Get glute and hamstring engagement before movement starts. One finger on the side of the glute, one at the hamstring belly.

Initiation: Reframe as a hip thrust — "Drive your hips through, like a hip thrust. Drive forward, don't lift up."

Points of Performance: Rib cage down throughout. Hand-block hyperextension at the top.

Trap Bar — Building Confidence with the Movement

Non-negotiable before every pull: big breath in, brace like someone's about to punch you, hold it through the whole rep. Pair with lat engagement cue and the setup check (hinge to grip, flat back confirmed before tension goes on). Side-on to see the spine. If the back rounds — weight comes down.

Coaching Presence — Speaking Up and Owning the Floor

Delivery is still too quiet and instructions sometimes come across as suggestions. The shift is in language and conviction: "Push your feet through the floor" instead of "Can you try to push your feet through the floor?" Same instruction — completely different impact. Use your body: demonstrate, use your hands, show them the position.

Positioning — Seeing Both Clients at Once

  • Benches in front of mirror — use the reflection to track your second client while with the first
  • Rep window — first several reps are the safe window; check the other client, call a cue, and come back

Conventional Deadlift — Joint Angles and Timing

Watch for two angles: the angle at the knee, and the angle between torso and thigh. Both should be opening at the same time as the lift progresses. If one lags behind the other — there's a disconnection, something's compensating.

Phase 3 vs Phase 4 — The Live Demo

A technically sound rep, then a rep where the bar moved A to B but everything else was wrong. The bar moving from A to B — that's Phase 3. How well it was executed — that's Phase 4. That's what we're coaching.

Rehab Exercises

If a client has rehab exercises programmed, they get done. These are typically physio-prescribed — the physio expects them completed as part of the client's recovery plan. Check at the start of the session, know what it is and why, and monitor the client through it. Don't let it get skipped.

Session Pacing

Follow the Perfect Session timing guidelines. The C series is your flex point. Most clients are on two sets — if you're running ahead, add a third. If you're running over, keep it at two and finish on time. A client should never be finishing their session early.

Reading Your Clients — Intensity and Load

Before every session, check the client's history in TeamBuilder. Use the warm-up as your diagnostic. If movement quality is strong, move toward their programme numbers. If something needs attention, scale back and explain why. Don't assume based on how someone looks — the history and the warm-up tell you what you need to know.

Core Exercise Coaching — Stability Before Mobilisation

Establish stability — core engagement, correct position, the right muscles switched on — before adding any load through movement or lever length.

Plank: Forearms flat, elbows under shoulders, hips in line. Brace before the hold begins. 2–3 cues in a 30-second hold. Side-on to see the hip line.

Hollow hold: Lower back staying flat is the non-negotiable. Find the point where it wants to come off the floor, then hold just above it. Regression: bend knees, arms by sides, one limb at a time.

Dead bugs: Arms vertical to ceiling, knees to tabletop, lower back flat before any limb moves. If the back arches, the rep stops. Slow the movement right down.

Sit-ups and crunches: Crunch is upper curl only, lower back stays on floor. Sit-up is full range, controlled descent. Common faults: pulling on the neck, using momentum.

Tactile Cueing

One point of contact, one direction, comfortable for both parties. A hand on the lower back gives feedback on whether they're maintaining floor contact. A light touch at the ribs helps them feel where the engagement should come from.

The Practical — Core Exercises

The big idea: stability before mobilisation.

  1. Get the client positioned and settled
  2. Activate the core before adding any movement or load — ribs tucked, brace engaged
  3. Then progress — elevate the legs, extend the lever, add difficulty

The hollow hold is the clearest example. If you ask someone to hold the hollow position without first getting the activation, their lower back takes the load instead of their core. The fix is simple: body at rest first, rib tuck, core braced, then legs go up.

Tactile cues: when verbal cues aren't landing — pick a specific body part and give it a direction. Not "brace your core" but "pull your lower ribs toward the floor." Keep it comfortable. Always tell the client before you make contact.

Hip Thrust — Getting the Setup Right

The key for the bench variation is shoulder blade position — shoulder blades need to rest above the edge of the bench, not over it. When they hang over the edge, the bench digs in and the position becomes uncomfortable.

Getting into position — the A-cam: Have the client place their elbows on the bench and press up to lift their hips. From there they shuffle back and position their shoulder blades correctly before settling into the start. Takes a bit of coaching, but once they feel the right position it makes sense.

The machine is more forgiving — it does some of the positioning work for you. The bench needs more active coaching before the rep even starts. And the soft box — now you know it's there and what it's for.
